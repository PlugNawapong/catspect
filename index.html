We started building multispectral imagers bit by bit, out of passion and curiosity. Every mistake and lesson learned made the next instrument simpler and better. The results kept looking promising, so we kept going, and now we're expanding.

01

2025 · Food safety · Published in Sensors

Portable nitrite imager

9 bands, 360–940 nmRaspberry Pi 53D-printedComputer vision

Our first 3D-printed prototype, with a nine-slot filter wheel, started with a food-safety problem. Sodium nitrite keeps processed meat safe, but too much of it is a health risk, and testing it properly needs a laboratory. We asked whether our imager could read a simple paper test pad and give a real number on the spot.

It could. Pairing the band where the test dye absorbs (550 nm) with a band where it doesn't (940 nm) gave a clean signal that held up under different lighting. Computer vision then sorted each pad into four concentration levels.

The question
Can we measure nitrite on-site, without a lab?
What we built
Our first 3D-printed imager with a nine-slot filter wheel, plus the analysis software
What we learned
Nine filters weren't enough for every task, and printed parts flexed and let in stray light.

02

2025 · Plant science · With the Faculty of Science, Mahidol University

Duckweed health study

Same prototype7 bands, 475–940 nm7 vegetation indicesAutomatic background removalHealth classes

Duckweed is a tiny floating plant that grows fast and reacts quickly to its environment, which makes it a useful model in plant biology. With plant scientists at Mahidol University, we pointed the same prototype at living plants, measuring duckweed health without touching the sample.

We captured seven bands from blue to near-infrared, then calculated seven vegetation indices, including NDVI, NDRE and a water index. The software removes the water background automatically, measures the area each frond covers, and labels every pixel as healthy, moderate or stressed.

The question
Can we judge duckweed health and coverage objectively, frond by frond?
What we built
New plant analysis on the same nine-slot prototype: indices, coverage and health
What we learned
Fronds that look the same green to the eye can differ clearly in near-infrared

03

2026 · Engineering upgrade

20-slot aluminium imager

Aluminium chassis2 × 10-slot CNC wheelsCATSPECT PCB V1NVIDIA edge AI

The prototype proved the science on plants and on food. Next we wanted an instrument that gives the same answer every time, lasts, and is ready for real-world use, so we rebuilt every part we had compromised on.

The housing became aluminium with a matte-black interior. One printed wheel became two machined wheels of ten slots each, for 20 filter positions. Loose wiring became our own custom circuit board, making the instrument more compact, and the processing moved to an NVIDIA edge-AI computer.

The question
Can the prototype become a precise, repeatable lab instrument?
What we built
Aluminium chassis, stacked CNC filter wheels, an eight-lamp lighting ring and our own PCB
Built alongside
A real-time version for samples that change while you watch
04

2026 · Real-time · Built alongside 03

Live plant-health imager

Red · Green · NIRNDVI · GNDVI · NNIRHealth classes
One live frame Healthy 61% Moderate 38% Unhealthy 1%

We developed this alongside the aluminium imager. Instead of rotating a filter wheel or switching lights, it reads red, green and near-infrared from every video frame. It computes vegetation indices on the fly and labels every pixel healthy, moderate or unhealthy.

Building on the duckweed study, we wanted the same health map without waiting for a scan. Watching it update in real time was one of the best moments of the project for all of us.

The question
Can we watch living samples change, rather than photograph them?
What we built
A real-time camera set-up and analysis software on the aluminium platform
Where it leads
Growth-chamber monitoring, crop stress and other live samples
