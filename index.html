Our story

It started for fun.

Three of us share one passion: building things that measure light. Ordinary cameras see only three broad colours, but much of what matters in food, plants and chemistry hides in narrower bands. We wanted to see them.

So we formed CATSPECT and started building, one part at a time, after hours and just for fun. The results looked promising, so we kept going.

Built by hand

From a table of parts to a working instrument.

We assemble every instrument ourselves. Here is how our aluminium imager came together, one step at a time.

  1. Step 1

    Lay out the parts

    Filters, retaining rings, machined wheels and the circuit board, checked and cleaned.

  2. Step 2

    Seat the filters

    Each filter is pressed into its slot and locked with a ring.

  3. Step 3

    Mount the camera

    The camera is fixed to the top plate, centred over the light path.

  4. Step 4

    Fit the wheels

    The two filter wheels go in under the camera, stacked in the light path.

  5. Step 5

    Add the lighting ring

    A machined ring with eight lamp mounts, for even light on the sample.

  6. Step 6

    Lock the lighting ring

    The ring is screwed tight inside the chassis, centred under the wheels.

  7. Step 7

    Close it up

    Panels on, cables in, electronics sealed.

  8. Step 8

    First light

    Lamps on, sample in, and the first test images.

01

2025 · Food safety · Published in Sensors

Portable nitrite imager

9 bands, 360–940 nmRaspberry Pi 53D-printedComputer vision

Sodium nitrite keeps processed meat safe, but too much of it is a health risk, and testing it properly needs a laboratory. We asked whether a small multispectral camera could read a simple paper test pad and give a real number on the spot.

It could. Pairing the band where the test dye absorbs (550 nm) with a band where it doesn't (940 nm) gave a clean signal that held up under different lighting. Computer vision then sorted each pad into four concentration levels.

The question
Can we measure nitrite on-site, without a lab?
What we built
A 1.4 kg imager with nine filters from UV to near-infrared, plus the analysis software
What we learned
Nine filters weren't enough for every task, and printed parts flexed and let in stray light.
+19.3%better fit (R²) than the best single band

02

2026 · Engineering upgrade

20-filter aluminium imager

Aluminium chassis2 × 10-slot CNC wheelsCATSPECT PCB V1NVIDIA edge AI

The prototype proved the science. Next we wanted an instrument that gives the same answer every time and lasts, so we rebuilt every part we had compromised on.

The housing became aluminium with a matte-black interior. One printed wheel became two machined wheels of ten slots each, for 20 filter positions. Loose wiring became our first circuit board, and the processing moved to an NVIDIA edge-AI computer.

The question
Can the prototype become a precise, repeatable lab instrument?
What we built
Aluminium chassis, stacked CNC filter wheels, an eight-lamp lighting ring and our own PCB
What we learned
Stepping through filters one by one is too slow for anything alive or moving.
03

2026 · Plant science · Real-time

Live plant-health imager

≈22 fpsRed · Green · NIRNDVI · GNDVI · NNIRHealth classes
One live frame FPS21.9 NDVI mean0.511 Healthy 61% Moderate 38% Unhealthy 1%

Instead of taking pictures one filter at a time, this instrument reads red, green and near-infrared from every video frame. It computes vegetation indices on the fly and labels every pixel healthy, moderate or unhealthy.

Our first live subject was duckweed, tiny floating plants used in plant-biology research. Watching their health map update in real time was one of the best moments of the project for all of us.

The question
Can we watch living samples change, rather than photograph them?
What we built
A real-time camera set-up and analysis software on the aluminium platform
Where it leads
Growth-chamber monitoring, crop stress and other live samples
